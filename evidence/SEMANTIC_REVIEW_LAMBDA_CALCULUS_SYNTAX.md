## Reverse-paraphrase checks

1. **Term alphabet and metavariables:** lambda terms are built from an infinite variable supply, the lambda symbol, and parentheses. The object variables `v_0`, `v_1`, and the ellipsis retain their source macros. Lowercase `x,y,z,...` designate variables, while uppercase `M,N,P,...` designate arbitrary terms.
2. **Inductive formation:** a variable is a term; if `x` is a variable and `M` a term, `(\lambd[x][M])` is a term; and if `M,N` are terms, `(MN)` is a term. The Bengali definition preserves all three hypotheses and conclusions without treating metavariables as object-language symbols.
3. **Abstraction and application:** a term formed by the second clause is the result of abstraction, and the `x` attached to its lambda is the parameter. A term formed by the third clause is the result of application. The explanatory paragraph and labels continue the Introduction chapter's abstraction/application register.
4. **Formation analysis:** the official syntax is fully parenthesized even though later conventions reduce the written burden. In the worked term, the last constructor is an abstraction with parameter `x`; its body is an application of two terms; and each component is itself an abstraction. The exercise preserves the complete self-application term whose formation is to be described.
5. **Starting-symbol lemmas:** a term begins either with a variable or with a parenthesis, because those exhaust the three formation clauses. An application begins with two parentheses or with a parenthesis followed by a variable: after its opening parenthesis, its left component is itself a term and therefore satisfies the first lemma.
6. **Proper initial parts:** no proper initial part of a term is itself a term. The exercise asks for induction on term length, and the proposition uses this exact prefix fact to rule out two different splits of an application into left and right components.
7. **Unique formation, variable case:** if `M` is a variable `x`, abstraction and application cannot have produced it because both begin with parentheses. Its only formation is therefore the single variable clause. Both explicit occurrences of `x` remain present in the target.
8. **Unique formation, abstraction case:** if `M` has form `(\lambd[x][N])`, it is neither a single variable nor an application. It can arise only by abstraction from `N`, whose own formation is unique by the induction hypothesis.
9. **Unique formation, application case:** if `M=(PQ)`, it cannot be a variable; and the starting-symbol lemma prevents `P` from beginning with lambda in a way that would make the whole term an abstraction. A second split `(P'Q')` would make one of `P,P'` a proper initial part of the other. The proper-initial-part lemma excludes this, so `P` and `Q`, and then their formations, are unique. Every `P` and `Q` explicitly typeset in the source remains explicitly typeset in Bengali.
10. **Three-way decomposition:** the restated proposition gives exactly three and only three term forms: a uniquely determined variable `x`; an abstraction with uniquely determined parameter `x` and body `N`; or an application with uniquely determined components `P,Q`. This is a readable equivalent of the formation proposition, not a new syntax.
11. **Abbreviation conventions:** omitting parentheses makes application associate from left to right, so `MNPQ` expands to `(((MN)P)Q)`. Lambda abstraction takes the widest possible scope, so `\lambd[x][MNP]` is one abstraction over the whole application string. A lambda with several variables abbreviates nested single-variable abstractions. Both displayed example directions and the exercise are formula-identical to the source.
12. **Binding intuition and shadowing:** in `\lambd[x][M]`, the binder captures free occurrences of `x` in `M`, while an inner `\lambd[x][N]` binds its own body's occurrences and shields them from the outer binder. The target preserves the occurrence-level distinction and does not imply that every written `x` is governed by the nearest outer abstraction indiscriminately.
13. **Scope correction:** if `\lambd[x][M]` occurs inside `N`, the governing lambda's scope is the corresponding occurrence of its body `M`. It is not all of `N`. BN-SRC-275 makes the definition agree with the constructor and the examples without changing any other symbol.
14. **Worked free/bound classifications:** in `\lambd[x][xy]`, `x` is bound and `y` is free. In `\lambd[x][xx]`, both `x` occurrences are bound because both are free in the body `xx`. In `((\lambd[x][xx])x)`, the last `x` is outside the abstraction and free. In `\lambd[x][(\lambd[x][x])x]`, the inner lambda binds the penultimate `x`, while the outer lambda binds the final `x`; the two scopes remain distinct.
15. **Free-variable recursion:** `FV(x)={x}`; `FV(\lambd[x][N])=FV(N)\setminus\{x\}`; and `FV(PQ)=FV(P)\cup FV(Q)`. These clauses respectively handle variables, removal of the newly bound parameter, and union across application components. All set braces, operators, labels, and metavariables are preserved.
16. **Exercises:** the first exercise asks for the scopes of `\lambd[g]` and both `\lambd[x]` occurrences in the same displayed term. The second asks whether every variable occurrence is bound and by which abstraction. The third asks for the free-variable set of the nested `x,y,z` term. The Bengali prompts retain these three distinct tasks.
17. **Environment dependence:** a free variable acts as a reference to an external environment. In `\lambd[x][fx]`, `f` is free, so the term's value depends on the environment's value for `f`. Abstracting over `f` yields `\lambd[f][\lambd[x][fx]]`, which takes both inputs explicitly and no longer depends on an environmental `f`. The translation preserves this semantic explanation without identifying an environment with a lambda term.
18. **Closed terms and the closing lemma:** a term with no free variables is a closed term, or combinator. For `y\ne x`, membership of `y` in `FV(\lambd[x][N])` is equivalent to membership in `FV(N)`; and membership in `FV(PQ)` is equivalent to membership in at least one component's free-variable set. The proof remains an exercise, followed by the explicit problem reference.
19. **Substitution motivation:** free variables refer to the environment, so replacing the free `f` in `\lambd[x][fx]` by the identity term yields `\lambd[x][(\lambd[y][y])x]`. The Bengali prose distinguishes this operation from replacing bound occurrences.
20. **Capture avoidance and shadowing:** substitution recurses through variables and applications. At an abstraction with the same binder as the substituted variable, the abstraction is unchanged. At a distinct binder, recursion is permitted only when the binder is absent from the replacement term's free variables; otherwise the operation is undefined because the binder would capture that variable. The three worked exercises retain every binder and replacement term.
21. **Free variables when the substituted variable is absent:** if `x\notin FV(M)` and the partial substitution is defined, then `FV(Subst(M,N,x))=FV(M)`. In the abstraction case, BN-SRC-276 supplies `x\notin FV(P)`, so the induction hypothesis applies to the actual body.
22. **Free variables when the substituted variable is present:** if `x\in FV(M)`, then a defined substitution has free-variable set `(FV(M)\setminus\{x\})\cup FV(N)`. The repaired abstraction calculation first transforms the body, removes binder `y`, and uses `y\notin FV(N)` to obtain the theorem's right side.
23. **Clearing and inversion:** if `Subst(M,N,x)` is defined and `x\notin FV(N)`, then `x` is absent from the result's free variables. If `Subst(M,y,x)` is defined and `y\notin FV(M)`, substituting `x` back for `y` returns `M`. Both induction calculations preserve the source application and abstraction forms.
24. **One-step alpha change:** changing a bound variable replaces an occurrence of `\lambd[x][N]` by `\lambd[y][Subst(N,y,x)]`, with distinctness, freshness, and definedness conditions. Compatibility closes the relation under abstraction and under either component of application. The three definitions are aligned after BN-SRC-278.
25. **Alpha conversion and equivalence:** alpha conversion is the smallest reflexive and transitive relation containing one-step change. The inverse lemma makes it symmetric, so the resulting relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. The examples correctly distinguish renaming a bound `x` from changing a free environmental `f` to `g`.
26. **Free-variable invariance:** one-step alpha change preserves the free-variable set in both cases `x\in FV(N)` and `x\notin FV(N)`; the other compatible-rule cases remain exercises. Symmetry of one-step change follows from the clearing and inverse-substitution theorems, and general alpha-equivalence therefore preserves free variables.
27. **Substitution modulo alpha-equivalence:** bound variables can be renamed so substitution is defined. The theorem's existence proof chooses fresh `z`, and its uniqueness part compares any second alpha-equivalent body and replacement. BN-SRC-280 and BN-SRC-281 ensure every comparison uses exactly the relation and premises proved above.
28. **Frozen duplicate exercise:** alpha-conversion exercise items 2 and 3 remain identical. Their formulas are preserved exactly and are not silently completed from conjecture.
29. **De Bruijn depth:** in `\lambd[x][\lambd[y][yx]]`, `y` lies zero intervening abstractions from its binder and `x` lies one away. The body therefore becomes `0\,1` and the full term becomes `\lambd[][\lambd[][01]]`. The target preserves both numbers and their order.
30. **Forward and inverse De Bruijn maps:** `F_\Gamma` looks up a variable's zero-based position, distributes over application, and pushes the new binder to the front of `\Gamma` under abstraction. `G_\Gamma` performs the inverse lookup and chooses a fresh variable under abstraction. The final proposition preserves that a one-step alpha change has identical `F_\Gamma` images whenever `\Gamma` contains every free variable of `M`.
31. **Classes and representatives:** from OLP-0364 onward, an unadorned uppercase metavariable denotes an alpha-equivalence class rather than a raw lambda term. `\rep{M}` denotes an arbitrary member, and indexed representatives permit several choices from the same class. The two worked alpha-equivalent abstractions remain in the same order.
32. **Operations on classes:** abstraction and application return the classes containing `\lambd[x][\rep{N}]` and `\rep{P}\rep{Q}`. Compatibility of alpha conversion makes these independent of representative choice. The target does not collapse the distinction between a representative and its class.
33. **Free variables and substitution on classes:** the free-variable set of a class is the set of any representative; alpha invariance makes it well-defined. Substitution returns the class containing the representative-level substitution. BN-SRC-287 supplies this necessary class codomain, while the cited corollary supplies independence from valid representative choices.
34. **Worked class substitution and Lambda-terms:** the example first chooses `\lambd[x][x]`, then the alpha-equivalent representative `\lambd[z][z]`, and completes the now-defined substitution. BN-SRC-288 removes only the empty equality. The resulting classes are called capital-lambda terms, and operations defined first on raw lambda terms are projected to them.
35. **Beta contraction and reduction:** beta contraction is the smallest compatible relation containing `(\lambd[x][N])Q \bredone \Subst{N}{Q}{x}`; such an application is a redex. Beta reduction is its reflexive-transitive closure, so it permits zero or more contraction steps. The problem still asks for the inductive presentation parallel to one-step bound-variable change.
36. **Normal forms and examples:** a beta-normal term has no further contraction, and a beta-normal reduct is a normal form. The examples preserve the terminating reduction to `y`, the growth sequence, the self-reproducing redex, and the two distinct contraction choices that both continue to `zv`.
37. **Reduction strategy:** the final common reduct motivates the Church--Rosser property without proving it here. The named natural strategy always contracts the left-most redex by starting position; the target preserves the iff claim that a normal form reachable by some strategy is reachable by this strategy.
38. **Beta equivalence:** the seven rules give reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, compatibility under left application, right application and abstraction, and the generating beta equation. The prose interpretation allows both contraction steps and their inverses. The later notation `\equal[X]` is retained for adding a rule `X`.
39. **Eta and beta-eta reduction:** eta contraction sends `\lambd[x][M x]` to `M` only when `x` is not free in `M`. Beta-eta reduction is the reflexive-transitive relation generated by both one-step beta and eta contractions. Every relation symbol and both inclusion rules are unchanged apart from the defined FV notation.
40. **Eta equivalence and extensionality:** BN-SRC-290 restores the general freshness-qualified eta equation before naming `\equal[\eta]`. Extensionality says that `Mx\equal Nx` implies `M\equal N` for an argument variable fresh for both terms, and names the extension `\equal[\ext]`. The Bengali term `ব্যাপ্তিগততা` continues the already translated First-order Logic heading.
41. **Equivalence of the extensions:** one direction abstracts an extensionality premise and eta-contracts both sides, showing eta equivalence is closed under extensionality. The other beta-contracts `(\lambd[x][Mx])x` to `Mx` and applies extensionality, deriving the eta equation. BN-SRC-291 keeps the final relation indexed by the defined extensionality macro.
