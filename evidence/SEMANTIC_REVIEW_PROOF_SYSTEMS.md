Reverse paraphrase for the introduction: a logic ordinarily has semantic notions such as truth, satisfiability, validity and entailment, together with a finite syntactic derivation system whose proposed derivations can be checked mechanically. Axiomatic systems are simple to state and study from the metatheoretic level but hard to construct and read. Natural deduction, truth trees or tableaux, sequents and resolution offer other finite arrangements, often trees that expose dependencies. Whatever formalism is chosen, theoremhood and derivability should coincide with validity and entailment. Soundness is the derivability-to-semantics direction; completeness is the converse. Syntactic consistency should likewise coincide with satisfiability, with the two directions corresponding to completeness and soundness in classical first-order logic.

Reverse paraphrase for sequents: a sequent is a pair of finite sentence sequences separated by the sequent sign; its left and right sequences may have independent lengths and either may be empty. A derivation is a tree whose top nodes are initial sequents or axioms and whose other nodes follow by rules that rearrange sentences or introduce a complex formula. Derivability from Gamma requires a root sequent with a finite sequence drawn from Gamma, while a theorem has an empty left side. Inconsistency is witnessed by a derivation with an empty right side, after which right weakening yields any sentence. BN-SRC-021 repairs the general displayed form: the frozen source ends both sequences at index m, while the Bengali formula ends the independent right sequence at n.

Reverse paraphrase for natural deduction: the system formalizes familiar mathematical patterns. Proof by cases derives one conclusion from each disjunct, indirect proof derives contradiction from the negated conclusion, and conditional proof derives the consequent under the antecedent. Each connective or quantifier has introduction and elimination rules. Some leaf assumptions are temporary: a rule may discharge them, so only the undischarged leaves remain dependencies of the conclusion. Gamma proves A when a derivation ends in A and every undischarged leaf belongs to Gamma; A is a theorem when all assumptions are discharged. The preserved example eliminates the conjunction A and B and then introduces the conditional, discharging the numbered conjunction assumption.

Reverse paraphrase for tableaux: a signed formula pairs a sentence with True or False, and a tableau expands such formulas down a branching tree. True conjunction adds both true components to one branch; false conjunction splits into a branch for each false component. A tableau closes when every branch contains matching true and false occurrences of one formula. Gamma proves A when some finite subset of Gamma, marked true together with A marked false, has a closed tableau. An inconsistent Gamma has a closed tableau whose every displayed true assumption comes from Gamma. Saturated open branches can be used to read off satisfying structures, and a closed tableau is closely related to an upside-down condensed sequent derivation.

Reverse paraphrase for axiomatic derivation: a derivation is a sentence sequence in which every line is supported as an axiom instance, a member of the premise set Gamma or the result of an inference rule. Axiom schemas describe fixed sentence forms and may be organized by the connective they govern or minimized, sometimes to one schema given a suitable primitive basis. Modus ponens supports B after earlier supported lines A and A conditional B. Gamma proves A when a supported sequence ends with A and its premise-supported lines are drawn from Gamma; theoremhood uses no such premises. The preserved three-line example uses two displayed axiom schemas and modus ponens. Inconsistency means proving falsity, and a complete axiom system then derives every sentence from an inconsistent set.
